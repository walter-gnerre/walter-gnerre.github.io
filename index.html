Carbon-Ceramic Evaporator

Solar Thermal Evaporative Propulsion (STEP) research
SHEET S-01

The evaporator is the core of our propulsion system: a carbon-ceramic absorber that turns sunlight into heat and evaporates water as propellant.

What I did

I fabricate and test the evaporators, and I wrote the lab's fabrication procedure.

How I did it
  • Fabricated more than 20 samples by hand at different concentrations of the carbon-ceramic material. The exact ratios are unpublished.
  • Wrote a step-by-step fabrication procedure so anyone in the group can reproduce a sample.
  • Tested samples with no light and at 1-sun equivalent on the lab's in-house solar simulator, about one hour per run.
  • Logged mass-loss rate, humidity, and simulator irradiance for every run.

SHEET S-02

What I did

I architected the spacecraft's software interface, wrote all 18 command and data handling (CDH) requirements, and was responsible for the power budget.

How I did it
  • Mapped every command and telemetry path between the onboard computer and 17 onboard components, grouped into 6 subsystems (FIG 2.1).

In-Situ Thrust Measurement

Making about 1 millinewton measurable in orbit
SHEET S-03

The mission's main goal is to measure the thruster's performance in orbit. After our first review we realized the original plan couldn't do it.

What I did

I worked with my team on a new way to measure thrust in orbit, and I built the 3U CubeSat render we used to explain it to reviewers.

How I did it
  • The problem: about 1 mN of thrust produces a velocity change buried in orbital perturbation noise, and weighing propellant loss directly isn't practical on a 3U bus.
  • Placed the thruster a known distance d from the spacecraft's center of mass, so thrust creates a torque that rotates the satellite instead of a nearly invisible push.
  • The IMU measures that rotation, and the reaction wheel counters it to hold attitude.
  • With the wheel torque known, thrust comes straight from the moment balance:
Στ ≈ 0 = τRW − F·d  → F = τRW / d
Result
~1mN
thrust made measurable in orbit
0
added sensors: reuses the ADCS IMU and reaction wheel
2
reviews defended after the change (SRR, PDR)

The mission's primary objective went from unmeasurable to measurable with hardware already on the spacecraft. Open at PDR: study the sun-pointing tolerance and pin down final thrust measurements. What I took from it: the limit wasn't the hardware, it was what we chose to measure.

Vacuum LED Solar Simulator

AM0 illumination for a NASA Kennedy thermal-vacuum test
SHEET S-04

To measure thrust directly, we're testing STEP inside a vacuum chamber at NASA's Kennedy Space Center in April 2027. I own the design of the solar simulator that will light it the way the Sun does in low Earth orbit.

What I did

I own the design of a vacuum-compatible LED solar simulator targeting 1361 W/m² (AM0) over a 10 × 10 cm target plane.

How I did it
  • Selected 14 candidate LEDs (365–945 nm) from published solar-simulator designs and digitized their datasheet spectra.
  • Built a Python tool to identify which LED combination most closely matches sunlight in low Earth orbit.
  • Designed for vacuum: with no air there's no convection, so the LEDs' waste heat leaves by conduction from a metal-core board into an aluminum baseplate and out through the chamber mount.
  • Produced the system block diagram and section view: power and signal through 2 chamber feedthroughs, with a photodiode and thermocouples verifying irradiance and temperature during the test.

Designed in OpenRocket, built by hand, flown on an H100W
SHEET S-05

My Level 1 certification rocket: 59.6 inches long, 3.1 inches in diameter, 33.5 ounces on the pad.

What I did

I designed, built, and flew a high-power rocket to earn my National Association of Rocketry Level 1 certification, and redesigned its four wooden fins along the way.

How I did it
  • Modeled the full rocket in OpenRocket: nose cone, body tube, coupler, bulkhead, a 36 in nylon parachute, and the H100W motor.

Untethered Robot Car

Simulated in TinkerCAD, built on an Arduino Nano ESP32
SHEET S-06

A two-motor robot car I wired and programmed from scratch, and the project that taught me the difference between a simulation and real hardware.

What I did

I built a 2-motor DC robot car that drives a preprogrammed sequence on battery power, with no computer attached.

How I did it
  • Prototyped the circuit and code in TinkerCAD with an Arduino Uno and a 9 V battery, since the simulator doesn't offer the Nano ESP32.
  • Built the real car on an Arduino Nano ESP32 with an L293D H-bridge driving 2 DC motors through 6 pins: 4 for direction, 2 for enable.
  • Wrote about 120 lines of C++: an 8-step open-loop sequence (forward, backward, left, right, each followed by a stop) that repeats every 11 seconds.
  • The simulation worked, but on the real car a 9 V battery couldn't drive both motors. Its low capacity and high internal resistance can't deliver the current two DC motors draw, so I switched to a 4×AA pack.

IREC Microgravity Payload

FIU SEDS, Spaceport America Cup 2027
SHEET S-07

I lead the payload for FIU's IREC 2027 rocket: a 3U-form-factor experiment on capillary-driven droplet ejection in microgravity.

What I did

I lead 10 students across 2 subteams (Research; Testing & Integration) and 2 FIU faculty labs, and I own the payload architecture.

How I did it
  • Conceptualized the experiment: compare how passive and self-propelled (Janus particle) colloids eject droplets from capillaries, using a 9-capillary matrix of 3 fluids × 3 replicates.
  • Defined 4 subsystems (structure, avionics, thermal, science), assigned owners, and set mass, power, and data budgets against IREC's payload rules.
  • Assessed 1–5% H₂O₂ against IREC hazard rules: identified ESRA's non-toxic classification, requested official confirmation, and drafted handling, storage, and PPE procedures.
  • After a platinum deposition failed and was traced to copper crucible contamination, began evaluating gold-coated, magnetically actuated particles as an H₂O₂-free alternative.

E.C.L.I.P.S.E. Dual-Rover Mission

NASA L'SPACE Mission Concept Academy
SHEET S-08

As Lead Systems Engineer I led eight engineers designing E.C.L.I.P.S.E., a two-rover mission to explore a permanently shadowed region (PSR) at the Moon's south pole.

What I did

I led the team and owned the mission requirements, concept of operations (CONOPS), and system architecture.

How I did it
  • Split the mission across two cooperative rovers. A PSR never sees sunlight and RTGs weren't allowed, so the mission had to run entirely on solar power collected outside the shadow.
  • Rim Station Unit: stays on the illuminated rim (about 41% average illumination), generates and stores solar power, talks to Earth, and relays power and data. It stays mostly stationary, rotating slightly to track the Sun.
  • Explorer Rover: drives into the PSR for in-situ science, tethered to the Rim Station for continuous power and data.
  • When stakeholders cut the mass budget 23% (150 → 115 kg combined), I ran a trade study across subsystems and brought the options back so the team decided together.
